% 1. ABSTRACT

\begin{abstract}

Large-scale vision--language models such as CLIP offer strong zero-shot
transfer, yet their performance on specialised visual domains---satellite
imagery, textures, fine-grained flowers---still lags behind lightweight
task-specific adaptation.  The question is not merely \emph{which adapter
is most accurate}, but \emph{how much accuracy each trainable parameter
buys}.

We present a controlled comparison of ten methods, spanning seven families
of adaptation strategy, applied to a \emph{single frozen} CLIP ViT-B/32
backbone:
(i)~zero-shot prompting and prompt ensembling,
(ii)~linear probing,
(iii)~Context Optimization (CoOp) with learned text prompts,
(iv)~CLIP-Adapter with a vision-side bottleneck MLP,
(v)~a joint CoOp\,+\,Adapter model that adapts both modalities simultaneously,
(vi)~Tip-Adapter and its fine-tuned variant Tip-Adapter-F with a training-free
key--value cache, and
(vii)~Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) injected into the vision tower.
As an exploratory baseline, we also evaluate Sinkhorn-based Optimal Transport
(OT) on patch-level tokens, which underperforms zero-shot on all benchmarks
and provides instructive negative results.

% TODO: Update numbers after final verified run
All methods share the same frozen backbone, the same evaluation loop, and the
same few-shot protocol across three datasets (EuroSAT, DTD, Flowers102),
making accuracy, parameter count, GPU memory, and wall-clock time directly
comparable.
Our main finding is that CoOp's 8\,192 learnable prompt tokens---0.0095\,\%
of the backbone---close the majority of the gap between zero-shot and
full supervision, while Tip-Adapter-F achieves the highest overall accuracy
with a fine-tuned visual cache.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Problem Statement and Motivation}

Contrastive Language--Image Pre-training (CLIP)~\cite{radford2021learning}
has demonstrated remarkable zero-shot transfer capabilities across
hundreds of visual recognition tasks.  By aligning images and captions in a
shared embedding space, CLIP enables classification via natural-language
prompts without any task-specific training.  Yet in specialised domains
---remote sensing, material textures, fine-grained botany---zero-shot
accuracy remains far from satisfactory: a frozen ViT-B/32 achieves only
46.4\,\% on EuroSAT~\cite{helber2019eurosat}, 54.9\,\% on
DTD~\cite{cimpoi2014describing}, and 71.5\,\% on
Flowers102~\cite{nilsback2008automated}, well below
the needs of any practical application.

The standard remedy is full fine-tuning, but updating all 86~million
parameters of a ViT-B/32 requires large labelled datasets and risks
catastrophic forgetting of the broad visual--linguistic knowledge that
makes CLIP useful in the first place.  A rapidly growing family of
\emph{parameter-efficient adaptation} methods addresses this by training a
small number of new parameters while keeping the backbone frozen.
These methods differ in \emph{where} they intervene---on the text side
(learned prompts), on the vision side (adapter layers), or on both---and
in \emph{how many parameters} they introduce, from zero (training-free
caches) to several hundred thousand (LoRA inside the backbone).

\subsection{Challenges and Prior Work}

Several adaptation strategies have been proposed for CLIP.
\textbf{CoOp}~\cite{zhou2022learning} replaces the hand-written text
template with $M$ continuous context vectors optimised by gradient descent.
\textbf{CLIP-Adapter}~\cite{gao2024clipadapter} appends a bottleneck MLP
after the frozen visual encoder and blends adapted features with the
originals via a residual connection.
\textbf{Tip-Adapter}~\cite{zhang2022tip} takes a training-free approach,
constructing a key--value cache from few-shot support images and combining
cache-based logits with zero-shot predictions.  Its fine-tuned variant,
\textbf{Tip-Adapter-F}, further optimises the cache keys by gradient
descent.
\textbf{LoRA}~\cite{hu2022lora}, originally proposed for large language
models, injects low-rank matrices into existing linear layers, enabling
adaptation \emph{inside} the backbone with a modest parameter overhead.
Finally, \textbf{Optimal Transport}~\cite{cuturi2013sinkhorn} offers a
principled framework for comparing distributions of tokens, using the
Sinkhorn algorithm to compute a structured distance between patch-level
image features and word-level text features.

Despite this rich landscape, existing studies typically evaluate each method
in isolation, with different backbones, different datasets, different shot
counts, and different hyperparameter protocols, making fair comparisons
difficult.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Comparisons}

These results are hard to compare with one another.  The backbones differ
(CoOp reports on a ResNet-50 CLIP, CLIP-Adapter on ViT-B/16, Tip-Adapter on
ViT-B/32, each under its own pre-training regime), and so do the shot
counts and sampling seeds, which confounds accuracy differences with data
selection effects.  Efficiency is rarely tabulated at all: papers report
accuracy but seldom GPU memory, training time, or trainable parameter count
measured on the same hardware, leaving practitioners without a basis for
cost--benefit analysis.  Negative results are largely absent, so methods
that underperform zero-shot CLIP---na\"ive Optimal Transport on token
features among them---go unpublished, and their failure modes with them.

\subsection{Contributions}

We implement ten adaptation strategies in a \textbf{single codebase}, all
sharing one frozen ViT-B/32 backbone (LAION-2B weights via OpenCLIP), one
evaluation loop, one few-shot sampler and one epoch budget, which makes
accuracy, parameter count, peak GPU memory and wall-clock time directly
comparable across every method.  On that basis we quantify the
\textbf{accuracy per trainable parameter} on three domains (EuroSAT, DTD,
Flowers102), finding that most of the zero-shot-to-supervised gap closes
with fewer than 10{,}000 parameters and that the marginal return falls
sharply beyond that point.  We build a \textbf{joint CoOp\,+\,CLIP-Adapter}
model that adapts both modalities at once, and document the software
engineering needed to compose two independently developed modules---MRO
conflicts, optimiser group splits, gradient-flow traps---without which the
joint model loses to either half alone.  We also report an
\textbf{informative negative result}, analysing why Sinkhorn Optimal
Transport on patch tokens fails to beat zero-shot CLIP.  The complete
codebase, experiment scripts and raw result files are publicly
available.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/MarcoEquisetto/APAI_exam}}

\subsection{Frozen Backbone}\label{sec:backbone}

All experiments use a ViT-B/32 backbone loaded from OpenCLIP with
LAION-2B weights (\texttt{laion2b\_s34b\_b79k}).  The backbone comprises
a 12-layer Vision Transformer (86\,M parameters) producing 512-dimensional
embeddings for both images and text.  All backbone parameters are frozen
(\texttt{requires\_grad\,=\,False}); only parameters introduced by each
adaptation module are trainable.

A shared base class, \texttt{BaseCLIPWrapper}, encapsulates the frozen
backbone and exposes two virtual methods---\texttt{get\_image\_features()}
and \texttt{get\_text\_features()}---that subclasses override to inject
their adapter logic.  CoOp, CLIP-Adapter, LoRA and the joint model each
override exactly one of the two and inherit the base
\texttt{predict()}, which recomputes both sets of features and scores them
by cosine similarity; the three methods whose decision rule is not a plain
cosine similarity---Tip-Adapter (cache logits added to the zero-shot
ones), Optimal Transport (a transport cost, minimised rather than
maximised) and the linear probe (a fitted classifier)---provide their own.
All of them are consumed by one evaluation loop, which is what makes the
accuracy, memory and timing columns comparable.

\subsection{Baseline Methods}\label{sec:baselines}

\paragraph{Zero-Shot CLIP.}
Classification is performed by encoding each class name with a prompt
template (\eg, ``a satellite image of \{class\}'') and selecting the class
whose text embedding has the highest cosine similarity to the image
embedding.  No parameters are trained.

\paragraph{Zero-Shot with Prompt Ensembling.}
Following Radford~\etal~\cite{radford2021learning}, we average the text
prototypes produced by 7~templates that vary in viewpoint and framing
(\eg, ``a satellite photo of \{\}'', ``an aerial view of \{\}'').  This
costs no additional parameters and typically adds 2--4 accuracy points.

\paragraph{Linear Probe.}
An $\ell_2$-regularised logistic regression (implemented via
\texttt{scikit-learn}) is fitted on the frozen image features.  The number
of trainable parameters is $C \times (D + 1)$, where $C$ is the number of
classes and $D = 512$.

\subsection{CoOp: Learnable Text Prompts}\label{sec:coop}

Context Optimization (CoOp)~\cite{zhou2022learning} replaces the
hand-written template with $M$ continuous context vectors in the text
transformer's input embedding space:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:coop}
  \mathbf{p}_c = [\mathrm{SOS}]\;
  \underbrace{\mathbf{v}_1\;\mathbf{v}_2\;\cdots\;\mathbf{v}_M}_{\text{learnable}}
  \;[\mathrm{tok}(c)]\;[\mathrm{EOS}]
\end{equation}
where $[\mathrm{tok}(c)]$ denotes the frozen BPE embedding of class name
$c$.  In the \emph{Unified Context} variant a single
$(\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_M)$ block is shared across all classes;
in the \emph{Class-Specific Context} (CSC) variant each class receives its
own block, multiplying the parameter count by $C$.

For ViT-B/32 with $M = 16$, the unified variant introduces
$16 \times 512 = 8{,}192$ trainable parameters.  The text transformer is
run from the assembled embeddings directly, bypassing the frozen token
embedding table for the context positions, so gradients flow only through
$\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_M$.  We train with SGD (momentum 0.9,
learning rate $2 \times 10^{-3}$) and a cosine-annealing schedule with a
linear warmup epoch.

\subsection{CLIP-Adapter: Vision-Side Bottleneck MLP}\label{sec:adapter}

CLIP-Adapter~\cite{gao2024clipadapter} appends a trainable bottleneck
MLP after the frozen vision encoder:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{adapt}} &= W_{\mathrm{up}} \;\sigma\bigl(
    W_{\mathrm{down}}\,\mathbf{f}\bigr) \label{eq:adapter-mlp}\\
  \tilde{\mathbf{f}} &= \alpha\,\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{adapt}}
    + (1-\alpha)\,\mathbf{f} \label{eq:adapter-blend}
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{f} \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the frozen, L2-normalised image
embedding, $W_{\mathrm{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{(D/r) \times D}$ and
$W_{\mathrm{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times (D/r)}$ form the bottleneck
with reduction ratio $r$, $\sigma$ is ReLU, and
$\alpha \in [0, 1]$ is a residual blending factor.
The adapted features $\tilde{\mathbf{f}}$ are L2-normalised before
similarity computation.

With $r = 4$ and $D = 512$ the adapter introduces $131{,}712$ trainable
parameters.  $W_{\mathrm{up}}$ is initialised near zero so that training
begins close to the original CLIP representation.  We train with AdamW
(learning rate $10^{-3}$, cosine schedule).

\subsection{Joint CoOp + CLIP-Adapter}\label{sec:joint}

The two methods are complementary by construction: CoOp overrides
\texttt{get\_text\_features()} and CLIP-Adapter overrides
\texttt{get\_image\_features()}.  We compose them in a single model that
adapts both modalities simultaneously, for a total of
$8{,}192 + 131{,}712 = 139{,}904$ parameters.

Naïve Python multiple inheritance fails because of a Method Resolution
Order (MRO) conflict: during construction,
\texttt{CLIPAdapterModel.\_\_init\_\_()} calls
\texttt{self.\_update\_text\_prototypes()}, which resolves to CoOp's text
path and reads \texttt{self.prompt\_learner}---a module that has not been
created yet.  We resolve this by constructing both modules explicitly in a
controlled order and assigning the feature methods as function attributes.

Crucially, the two halves require different optimiser recipes.  CoOp trains
with SGD at $2 \times 10^{-3}$; the adapter trains with AdamW at
$10^{-3}$.  A single learning rate applied to both degrades accuracy
below either method alone.  We therefore define per-module parameter groups
under one AdamW optimiser.

\subsection{Tip-Adapter and Tip-Adapter-F}\label{sec:tip}

Tip-Adapter~\cite{zhang2022tip} constructs a training-free key--value
cache $(\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{train}}, \mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{train}})$ from
the few-shot support set, where $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{train}} \in
\mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ are frozen image features and
$\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{train}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C}$ are one-hot
label vectors.  At test time:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tip}
\begin{split}
  \mathrm{logits} = {}& 100\,\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{test}}
    \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{text}}^\top \\
  &+ \alpha\,\exp\!\bigl(-\beta(1 - \mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{test}}
    \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{train}}^\top)\bigr)\,\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{train}}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where the first term is the zero-shot logit and the second is a
cache-based correction weighted by visual similarity.

\textbf{Tip-Adapter-F} promotes the cache keys $\mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{train}}$
to learnable parameters and fine-tunes them with cross-entropy loss (AdamW,
$\mathrm{lr} = 10^{-3}$), under the same 10-epoch budget as every other
trainable method.  The number of trainable parameters is $N \times D$,
where $N = K \cdot C$ is the size of the support set: this is the only
method whose cost grows with the number of classes, reaching 522{,}240
parameters on Flowers102 against 81{,}920 on EuroSAT.

\subsection{LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation Inside the Backbone}\label{sec:lora}

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\cite{hu2022lora} injects trainable low-rank
matrices into existing linear layers.  For each target layer with weight
$W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, LoRA
adds:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lora}
  W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,B\,A
\end{equation}
where $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and
$B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}} \times r}$ with rank $r \ll
\min(d_{\mathrm{in}}, d_{\mathrm{out}})$; $\alpha$ scales the
contribution.  $W_0$ remains frozen.

We apply LoRA to the MLP layers (\texttt{c\_fc} and \texttt{c\_proj}) of
every transformer block in the vision tower with $r = 4$ and
$\alpha = 1.0$, introducing $368{,}640$ trainable parameters.  Unlike all
other methods in this study, LoRA modifies the computation \emph{inside}
the backbone; the pretrained weights still remain frozen, but this is a
deliberate out-of-brief comparison.  We train with AdamW at
$\mathrm{lr} = 10^{-4}$ (10$\times$ lower than the adapter) with cosine
scheduling.

\subsection{Optimal Transport (Exploratory)}\label{sec:ot}

As an exploratory baseline, we use Sinkhorn-based Optimal
Transport~\cite{cuturi2013sinkhorn} to compute structured distances between
the full set of image patch tokens and text word tokens.  For an image with
$P$ patch tokens $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}$ and a class with $T$ text tokens
$\{\mathbf{y}_j\}$, we build a cost matrix
$C_{ij} = 1 - \cos(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{y}_j)$ and solve:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sinkhorn}
  d_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})
    = \min_{\gamma \in \Pi(\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b})}
      \sum_{ij} \gamma_{ij}\,C_{ij}
      + \varepsilon\,\mathrm{KL}(\gamma \,\|\, \mathbf{a} \otimes \mathbf{b})
\end{equation}
with uniform marginals $\mathbf{a} = \frac{1}{P}\mathbf{1}$ and
$\mathbf{b} = \frac{1}{T}\mathbf{1}$, regularisation $\varepsilon = 0.05$,
and maximum 100 Sinkhorn iterations.  The predicted class is the one with
the lowest transport cost.  No parameters are trained.

\subsection{Datasets}\label{sec:datasets}

We evaluate on three benchmarks spanning distinct visual domains:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{EuroSAT}~\cite{helber2019eurosat}: 27{,}000 Sentinel-2
    satellite images, 10 land-use classes.  Template: ``a satellite image
    of \{class\}''.
  \item \textbf{DTD}~\cite{cimpoi2014describing}: 5{,}640 texture images,
    47 classes.  Template: ``a photo of a \{class\} texture''.
  \item \textbf{Flowers102}~\cite{nilsback2008automated}: 8{,}189 flower
    images, 102 fine-grained categories.  Template: ``a photo of a
    \{class\}, a type of flower''.
\end{itemize}

All images are resized to $224 \times 224$ with bicubic interpolation and
normalised with CLIP's own channel statistics (mean
$(0.481, 0.458, 0.408)$, std $(0.269, 0.261, 0.276)$), matching the
preprocessing used during pre-training.  No data augmentation is applied.

\subsection{Training Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

All trainable methods draw from the same seeded, class-balanced few-shot
support set, share a matched budget of 10 epochs, use batch size 64, and
follow a cosine-annealing learning rate schedule with a 1-epoch linear
warmup.  The optimiser recipe stays the one prescribed by each method's
paper---SGD with momentum for CoOp, AdamW for the adapter, the joint model
and LoRA---since the optimiser is part of the method whereas the epoch
budget is not.

Two caveats about uniformity.  First, the support set is capped by what the
training split actually contains: the official Flowers102 training split
holds exactly 10 images per class, so a request for 16 shots yields
\emph{10} shots on that dataset while EuroSAT and DTD supply the full 16.
Second, Tip-Adapter-F does not run through the shared training loop---it
optimises its cache keys directly with AdamW, so it receives the same
10-epoch budget but no warmup and no cosine schedule.

Experiments are run on an NVIDIA GPU with PyTorch~2.5.1 (CUDA~12.1).

\paragraph{Key observations.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prompt ensembling is free.}  Averaging text features across
    seven templates adds $+$4.3 points on EuroSAT and $+$2.4 on DTD at
    zero parameter cost, establishing a stronger zero-shot baseline.  The
    gain is much smaller on Flowers102 ($+$0.5), where the single template
    ``a photo of a \{\}, a type of flower'' already carries the domain
    information the other templates add elsewhere.

  \item \textbf{CoOp closes the gap efficiently.}  With only 8{,}192
    parameters (0.0095\,\% of the backbone), CoOp achieves 75.87\,\% on
    EuroSAT---a $+$29.5 point improvement over zero-shot---and its
    88.71\,\% on Flowers102 surpasses even the linear probe.  The vision
    adapter, with sixteen times as many parameters, buys $+$5.4 more on
    EuroSAT and $+$1.9 on DTD, where the visual domain shift is largest;
    combining the two adds nothing beyond the better half.

  \item \textbf{Tip-Adapter-F wins on two datasets out of three.}
    Fine-tuning the visual cache achieves the highest accuracy on EuroSAT
    (86.26\,\%) and Flowers102 (94.02\,\%).  On DTD it places third
    (73.46\,\%), behind CLIP-Adapter (74.31\,\%) and the joint model
    (74.15\,\%)---and it is also the method whose parameter count grows
    fastest with the number of classes, from 81{,}920 on EuroSAT to
    522{,}240 on Flowers102.

  \item \textbf{LoRA is competitive on EuroSAT but lags elsewhere.}
    Despite having the most parameters (368{,}640), LoRA does not
    generalise as well as lighter methods on DTD and Flowers102, likely
    due to overfitting on the few-shot training set.

  \item \textbf{Optimal Transport fails.}  The Sinkhorn method scores far
    \emph{below zero-shot} on every dataset---20.3\,\% against 46.4\,\% on
    EuroSAT, and 19.1\,\% against 71.5\,\% on Flowers102.  It stays well
    above chance (0.98\,\% for 102 classes), so the token-level signal is
    not absent, merely much weaker than the pooled one.
    We analyse this failure in Section~\ref{sec:ot-analysis}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Training Cost and Resource Usage}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} summarises training time; peak memory is reported in
Table~\ref{tab:main}.
Tip-Adapter-F trains in 44--146\,s because gradients touch only the cache
keys, never the backbone; CoOp and CLIP-Adapter require 265--713\,s
depending on the dataset; the joint model is the slowest of the
gradient-based methods, its time dominated by the CoOp-side backward pass
through the text transformer.

\paragraph{Memory does not discriminate.}
Peak allocation separates the methods far less than accuracy or time does.
The four zero-training baselines sit at 738--749\,MB and every trainable
method between 1{,}327 and 1{,}395\,MB: a spread of under 70\,MB on a
1.4\,GB footprint, because the frozen backbone and the activations of a
64-image batch dominate in every configuration.  Even LoRA, which
back-propagates through all twelve visual blocks, adds only about 40\,MB
over CoOp.  The resource axis that actually separates these methods is
\emph{time}, not memory---which is why Table~\ref{tab:cost} exists.

\subsection{Analysis of Optimal Transport Failure}\label{sec:ot-analysis}

The Sinkhorn method under-performs zero-shot CLIP on every dataset.  We
identify three structural reasons:

\textbf{Misaligned feature spaces.}  We do apply the output projection to
all tokens, but the contrastive objective only ever trained the pooled
[CLS] token to align with text; individual patch tokens inhabit a space
that was never directly optimised for text matching.
\textbf{Uninformative marginals.}  Uniform weights treat background patches
(sky, soil, water) as equal in importance to object-relevant ones, and in
satellite imagery, where the object of interest often occupies a small part
of the $7 \times 7$ grid, the background dominates the transport plan.
\textbf{Template-word dominance.}  Each class prompt shares its template
words (``a'', ``satellite'', ``image'', ``of''), 5 of the $\sim$7
non-padding tokens, so the Sinkhorn distance is dominated by the cost of
aligning patches to tokens that carry no class information at all---a
problem that grows worse, not better, as the prompt gets more descriptive.

These insights suggest concrete improvements (attention-weighted marginals,
class-name-only tokens) that we leave for future work.

\paragraph{Learning rate: a step, not a peak.}
The response is a step function rather than the usual inverted-U.  Below
$2\times10^{-5}$ the context vectors barely move within the budget and
accuracy collapses to 58.6\,\%; from $2\times10^{-4}$ upwards, four orders
of magnitude of learning rate---up to $2\times10^{-1}$---land within one
point of each other.  The nominal optimum is $2\times10^{-4}$ (86.83\,\%),
but its 0.20-point margin over the $2\times10^{-3}$ of the original CoOp
recipe is smaller than the run-to-run spread we measure elsewhere
(Section~\ref{sec:adapter-ablation}), so we keep $2\times10^{-3}$ for the
main grid.  With only 8{,}192 parameters and gradient norms of order
$10^{3}$, the warmup epoch is not optional: without it training diverges
at every learning rate above $10^{-3}$.

\paragraph{Context length: flat.}
Quadrupling the context from $M{=}4$ to $M{=}16$ buys 0.32 points
(86.31 $\to$ 86.63).  The extra capacity is neither wasted nor useful; at
this support-set size the bottleneck is evidently not the expressiveness
of the prompt.

\paragraph{Shots: the only axis that moves.}
Accuracy climbs monotonically with supervision, from 69.8\,\% at one shot
to 86.6\,\% at sixteen---a 17-point range, where the other three axes
together span barely three.  The one inversion, $K{=}2$ scoring below
$K{=}1$, is an artefact of single-seed sampling: with two images per class
the identity of those images matters more than their number, and we did
not average over seeds.  We flag it rather than smooth it over.

\subsubsection{Unified vs.\ Class-Specific Context (CSC)}

The standard few-shot expectation is that class-specific context
overfits: $C \times M \times D$ parameters are a lot to fit on $K \times C$
images, and the original CoOp paper recommends unified context in the
low-shot regime.  Our EuroSAT runs say the opposite.  CSC reaches
89.09\,\% against the 86.63\,\% of unified context---$+2.46$ points, by
some distance the largest effect in Table~\ref{tab:coop-ablation} outside
the shot count, and an order of magnitude larger than the noise floor
established in Section~\ref{sec:adapter-ablation}.

The price is ten times the parameters (81{,}920 against 8{,}192), which
still leaves CSC below the CLIP-Adapter budget while beating it on this
dataset.  We read the result as a property of EuroSAT rather than a
refutation of the original recommendation: with only ten land-use classes,
each class receives 16 images \emph{and} its own 16 context vectors, a
ratio far more favourable than the 102-class benchmarks CoOp was tuned on.
Confirming that reading would require repeating the sweep on DTD and
Flowers102, which we leave to future work.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

We have presented a unified, controlled comparison of ten methods---seven
families of parameter-efficient adaptation, plus two zero-shot baselines
and an exploratory Optimal Transport variant---evaluated on three visually
diverse benchmarks with a common frozen ViT-B/32 backbone.
Four findings stand out.  \textbf{Extreme parameter efficiency is
achievable}: CoOp's 8{,}192 context tokens, under 0.01\,\% of the backbone,
recover a substantial fraction of the zero-shot-to-supervised gap, and the
sixteen-fold larger adapter adds only a few points on top.
\textbf{Tip-Adapter-F offers the best accuracy--cost trade-off}, taking the
highest accuracy on two datasets out of three while training in a fraction
of the time gradient-based methods need---though it is also the method
whose cost grows with the number of classes.  \textbf{Joint text--vision
adaptation yields marginal gains}: the combined model lands within
0.25 points of the better of its halves on all three datasets ($+0.33$ on
EuroSAT, $-0.16$ on DTD, $-0.23$ on Flowers102), which suggests the two
exploit overlapping subspaces of the representation gap---and reaching even
that parity required a separate optimiser group per module, without which
the joint model lost up to 7.6 points.  Finally, \textbf{token-level
Optimal Transport fails without careful engineering}, for the three
structural reasons analysed in Section~\ref{sec:ot-analysis}.

For practitioners adapting CLIP to a specialised domain without the cost of
full fine-tuning, the practical ordering is: start with prompt ensembling,
which is free; add CoOp once labels are available; and consider
Tip-Adapter-F when accuracy matters more than a parameter count that scales
with the label set.

\subsection{Limitations}

Three limitations affect how our numbers should be read.  There is
\textbf{no validation split}: we select per-epoch checkpoints and tune
hyperparameters on the test set, as is common in the few-shot CLIP
literature, so every accuracy we report is an optimistic estimate.  Every
number also comes from a \textbf{single seed} (\texttt{seed=0}); the only
run-to-run spread we can measure---0.06 points between two runs of the same
adapter configuration---is smaller than the effects we discuss, but
differences of a few tenths should not be read as meaningful, and the
$K{=}2$ inversion in Table~\ref{tab:coop-ablation} is a direct consequence.
And the \textbf{few-shot protocol is not uniform}: the official Flowers102
training split holds 10 images per class, so our ``16-shot'' setting is in
fact 10-shot there, against the full 16 on EuroSAT and DTD.

Three more bound what the results cover.  The \textbf{ablations run on a
different protocol} from the main grid---the CoOp sweeps use 100 epochs,
the CLIP-Adapter sweeps the full training split, both on EuroSAT only---so
they are internally consistent but not comparable with Table~\ref{tab:main}
nor with each other.  We use a \textbf{single backbone} (ViT-B/32) with
\textbf{LAION-2B weights} (\texttt{laion2b\_s34b\_b79k}) rather than the
original OpenAI checkpoint, which shifts the zero-shot baselines and leaves
open whether the trade-offs hold at ViT-L/14 scale.  Finally, we apply
\textbf{no data augmentation}, unlike the original CoOp and CLIP-Adapter
papers.

\subsection{Future Work}

Three of our limitations point directly at the work that would remove
them: a held-out validation split to decouple model selection from test
evaluation, standard augmentations (random resized crop, horizontal flip)
to match the original papers' protocols, and a repeat of the whole grid on
ViT-L/14 to test whether these trade-offs hold at scale.  Two findings
point further out.  The Optimal Transport failure is specific enough to be
actionable---attention-derived marginals in place of uniform ones, and text
tokens restricted to the class name---and would show whether token-level
matching is fundamentally unsuited to CLIP or merely mis-specified here.
And the CSC result, if it survives on DTD and Flowers102, would sit
naturally alongside conditional context
generation~\cite{zhou2022conditional}, which makes the context a function
of the image rather than of the class.
